# Naval Classifier: APS5 Edition

## Elevator Pitch

**Naval Classifier: APS5 Edition** is a mobile-first, semi-satirical classification game about the heroic administrative burden of looking at boats and deciding what they probably are before they sail past your underfunded observation post.

You play as an **APS5 Naval Classifier**, a mid-level government specialist armed with a laminated recognition guide, a suspiciously slow tablet, and an inbox full of contradictory policy updates. Your job is simple: classify each vessel as it passes.

At first, it is easy.

A patrol boat is a patrol boat. A submarine is a submarine. A ferry is a ferry.

Then the sea gets weird.

By the end, you are confidently distinguishing between a “ceremonial tugboat,” a “strategic inflatable,” a “budget-neutral aircraft carrier,” and “three kayaks in a trench coat,” while senior leadership sends urgent emails asking why your accuracy is down 2% during a fog event.

## Tone and Style

The game should feel like a joyful collision between:

- A naval recognition training simulator.
- A government workplace satire.
- A mobile arcade reflex game.
- A bureaucratic fever dream.

The humour is affectionate rather than mean-spirited. The player is not mocked for failing; the systems around them are ridiculous. The ocean is full of nonsense, and somehow the APS5 is still expected to produce clean data.

## Player Fantasy

The fantasy is not “be an admiral.”

The fantasy is:

> “I am a strangely competent public servant doing impossible work with a broken process, and somehow I am becoming terrifyingly good at identifying boats.”

The player should feel:

- Increasingly skilled.
- Increasingly overwhelmed.
- Constantly amused by escalating absurdity.
- Mildly judged by performance dashboards.
- Proud when they correctly classify something completely deranged.

## Core Gameplay Loop

Each round presents a vessel sailing across the screen from one side to the other. The player has a short window to classify it before it exits.

1. A vessel appears on the horizon.
2. The player studies its silhouette, labels, flags, attachments, behaviour, or absurd visual clues.
3. Classification options appear as large mobile-friendly buttons.
4. The player taps the best classification.
5. The game provides immediate feedback:
   - Correct classification.
   - Explanation.
   - Points awarded.
   - Optional bureaucratic commentary.
6. The next vessel appears, usually slightly faster, stranger, or more ambiguous.

The core interaction must be playable one-handed on a phone. Sessions should work in short bursts of 2–5 minutes.

## Round Structure

### Phase 1: Basic Recognition

The player begins with straightforward vessel types:

- Patrol boat.
- Fishing boat.
- Sailboat.
- Ferry.
- Cargo ship.
- Submarine.
- Tugboat.

The art is clear and readable. The options are sensible. The game teaches the fundamentals: silhouette, scale, speed, flags, and visible equipment.

### Phase 2: Operational Ambiguity

The classifications become more specific and the vessels less cooperative:

- Survey vessel.
- Customs launch.
- Research trawler.
- Amphibious transport.
- Mine countermeasure vessel.
- Offshore support vessel.
- Decommissioned frigate now used for “community engagement.”

Weather, lighting, and UI pressure begin to interfere. The player receives memos that subtly change classification rules.

### Phase 3: Bureaucratic Escalation

The game begins to parody institutional classification systems:

- Legacy patrol asset.
- Temporarily reclassified training hull.
- Capability-adjacent maritime platform.
- Vessel of uncertain but funded significance.
- Boat, general purpose, pending review.
- Contractor-operated nautical deliverable.

The player may need to classify based on tiny clues like a procurement sticker, a ministerial photo opportunity banner, or whether the vessel has been renamed three times.

### Phase 4: The Sea Becomes Impossible

The game becomes proudly ridiculous while still internally consistent:

- Three kayaks in a trench coat.
- Budget-neutral aircraft carrier.
- Emotionally unavailable submarine.
- Amphibious meeting room.
- Sovereign citizen houseboat.
- Ceremonial tugboat with strategic intent.
- Decoy decoy vessel.
- Inflatable destroyer, probably.
- The same patrol boat wearing glasses.
- Unidentified floating KPI.

The trick is that absurd vessels should still have learnable patterns. The joke lands best when the player thinks, “Unfortunately, yes, that is clearly a ceremonial tugboat.”

## Classification Categories

The game can use several layers of classification depth.

### Primary Class

The broad vessel type:

- Civilian.
- Military.
- Commercial.
- Government.
- Unknown.
- Technically a boat.

### Vessel Type

The functional category:

- Patrol.
- Transport.
- Fishing.
- Research.
- Support.
- Submarine.
- Amphibious.
- Recreational.
- Administrative anomaly.

### Confidence Rating

A secondary input that lets players gamble:

- Low confidence.
- Medium confidence.
- High confidence.
- I would brief this to a committee.

Higher confidence multiplies score but increases penalty if wrong.

### Special Tags

Optional late-game modifiers:

- Flying the wrong flag.
- Clearly over budget.
- Probably a contractor.
- Contains admiral.
- Has no business being here.
- Looks classified but is actually a ferry.
- Is a ferry but somehow classified.

## Mobile UX Principles

The game should be built for phones first.

### Screen Layout

Top area:

- Score.
- Time remaining.
- Current shift status.
- Accuracy percentage.

Middle area:

- Animated ocean lane.
- Vessel silhouette or illustration.
- Weather and environmental effects.

Bottom area:

- Large classification buttons.
- Confidence selector.
- Optional special tag button.

### Interaction Design

- Large tap targets.
- No tiny text during timed moments.
- Portrait orientation by default.
- One-handed play.
- Haptic feedback for correct, wrong, and “bureaucratically correct” answers.
- Fast restart after failure.
- Readable in sunlight-like contrast.

### Session Length

- Quick shift: 2 minutes.
- Standard shift: 5 minutes.
- Endless overtime: continues until the player is overwhelmed or promoted sideways.

## Progression

The player advances through public-service-flavoured ranks and responsibilities.

### Career Track

1. APS4 Acting Classifier.
2. APS5 Naval Classifier.
3. APS5 Senior Naval Classifier, unofficial.
4. APS6 Maritime Recognition Coordinator.
5. EL1 Director, Boat Uncertainty.
6. EL2 First Assistant Secretary for Floating Things.
7. External Consultant.

Promotion does not necessarily make the game easier. It mostly adds more dashboards, more ambiguous categories, and more meetings.

### Unlocks

Players can unlock:

- New vessel packs.
- New sea regions.
- New weather effects.
- New absurd policy memos.
- New classifier manuals.
- Cosmetic desk items.
- Alternative UI themes, such as “Outdated Defence Tablet” or “Emergency Ministerial Briefing Mode.”

## Scoring

The scoring system rewards speed, accuracy, confidence, and streaks.

### Base Score

- Correct classification: points awarded.
- Faster response: bonus points.
- Correct confidence selection: bonus points.
- Correct special tag: bonus points.

### Penalties

- Wrong classification: score reduction.
- Overconfident wrong answer: larger penalty.
- Missed vessel: incident report.
- Repeated misclassification: mandatory training module.

### Special Outcomes

Not every answer is simply right or wrong.

- **Correct:** The vessel was identified properly.
- **Incorrect:** The vessel was misidentified.
- **Bureaucratically Correct:** Technically wrong in reality, but correct according to the latest memo.
- **Operationally Useful:** Not the exact class, but good enough to prevent a meeting.
- **Escalated:** The answer is so uncertain it has been forwarded to someone on leave.

## Difficulty Escalation

Difficulty should rise in several overlapping ways:

- Faster vessels.
- More similar silhouettes.
- Bad weather.
- Night mode.
- Fog.
- Glare.
- Distracting radio chatter.
- Contradictory rule updates.
- Decoy vessels.
- Vessels partially obscured by waves.
- Multiple vessels at once.
- Classification options with annoyingly similar names.

The game should avoid becoming unfair. Ridiculous does not mean random. Every vessel should have a clue, even if the clue is silly.

## Example Vessels

### HMAS Definitely Not A Ferry

A large grey vessel with passenger windows painted over and a suspiciously official crest.

Correct classification:

- Government transport, reclassified for optics.

Common wrong answers:

- Ferry.
- Patrol vessel.
- Public-private maritime initiative.

Feedback:

> “Correct. It was a ferry last financial year, but the new paint scheme has changed the reporting category.”

### The Strategic Inflatable

A small inflatable boat with an enormous radar mast and a defence contractor logo.

Correct classification:

- Capability-adjacent maritime platform.

Common wrong answers:

- Dinghy.
- Special forces craft.
- Procurement incident.

Feedback:

> “Bureaucratically correct. While visually a dinghy, it has been funded as a platform.”

### Three Kayaks in a Trench Coat

Three kayaks stacked vertically under a long canvas cover.

Correct classification:

- Composite low-signature paddled formation.

Common wrong answers:

- Unknown vessel.
- Smuggling craft.
- Performance art.

Feedback:

> “Correct. The formation was detected during the 2027 kayaking capability review.”

### The Emotionally Unavailable Submarine

A submarine that dives whenever classification options appear.

Correct classification:

- Submarine, avoidant.

Common wrong answers:

- Whale.
- Classified program.
- Absence of boat.

Feedback:

> “Correct. It has declined to surface for stakeholder engagement.”

## Satirical Systems

### Policy Memos

Between rounds, the player receives short memos that alter the rules.

Examples:

- “All vessels with banners are to be considered community engagement assets until further notice.”
- “Due to reporting harmonisation, tugboats may now be classified as strategic enablers.”
- “Please avoid using the term ‘unknown.’ Preferred wording is ‘classification opportunity.’”
- “Any vessel observed near a minister is to be treated as operationally significant.”

### Performance Reviews

At the end of each shift, the player receives a review.

Metrics include:

- Classification accuracy.
- Average response time.
- Confidence calibration.
- Memo compliance.
- Incident reports generated.
- Number of boats promoted to platforms.

Performance comments should be dry and absurd:

> “You demonstrated strong classification agility, though your reluctance to identify fishing vessels as strategic assets has been noted.”

### The Inbox

An optional meta-progression feature where the player receives emails from fictional departments:

- Capability Assurance.
- Maritime Terminology Reform.
- Office of Boat-Like Objects.
- Directorate of Ambiguous Hulls.
- The Minister’s Media Unit.

The inbox can deliver jokes, unlock rules, and introduce new vessel categories.

## Visual Direction

The visual style should be bright, readable, and charming.

Recommended art direction:

- Clean 2D silhouettes.
- Slightly exaggerated shapes.
- Animated waves.
- Big readable vessel features.
- Playful UI inspired by government dashboards.
- Stamp-like feedback animations: “CLASSIFIED,” “RECHECK,” “ESCALATED,” “BUREAUCRATICALLY CORRECT.”

The game should look polished but not overly realistic. The humour works best when the visuals are crisp and official-looking while the content is absurd.

## Sound Direction

Sound should be light, tactile, and satirical.

Possible sounds:

- Soft sonar pings.
- Office notification chimes.
- Stamp thunks.
- Distant gulls.
- Radio static.
- Printer noises for incident reports.
- A subtle dramatic sting when a vessel is “escalated.”

Music should feel like a calm government training video slowly losing control.

## Game Modes

### Training Shift

A tutorial mode with clear vessels and friendly feedback.

### Standard Shift

The main timed mode. Classify as many vessels as possible during a shift.

### Overtime Mode

Endless mode. The game gets harder until the player fails too many classifications or gets reassigned to a working group.

### Audit Mode

A challenge mode where previous classifications are reviewed under new policy guidance. The player must reclassify old vessels using revised definitions.

### Ministerial Visit

A special event mode where every vessel is suddenly politically important and the classification categories become more flattering.

## Monetisation Philosophy

If monetised, the game should avoid pay-to-win mechanics.

Good options:

- Premium one-time purchase.
- Cosmetic packs.
- Additional themed vessel packs.
- Optional expansion: “Procurement Season.”
- Optional expansion: “Fog of Governance.”

Avoid:

- Energy timers.
- Loot boxes.
- Ads between every round.
- Paid accuracy boosts.

## Minimum Lovable Product

The first fantastic version should include:

- A mobile-first playable classification loop.
- 30 vessels across escalating absurdity tiers.
- 4 classification options per vessel.
- Timed rounds.
- Score, streak, and accuracy tracking.
- End-of-shift performance review.
- Satirical feedback text.
- Local high score storage.
- Cloudflare Pages deployment readiness.

## North Star

The game succeeds if players start by laughing at the premise, then unexpectedly care about getting good at it.

The ideal player reaction is:

> “This is ridiculous. Obviously that is a capability-adjacent maritime platform. Give me the next boat.”
